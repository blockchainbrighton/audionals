# Chapter 20: Event One


The Control Room of the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) did not look like the bridge of a starship. It looked, James thought, more like the back office of a chaotic taxi firm, albeit one with an unlimited electricity budget.

The air was thick with a blue haze of cigarette smoke, despite the ventilation humming aggressively overhead. Discarded coffee cups formed jagged skylines on the consoles, and the floor was a tangle of thick black cables that threatened to trip anyone foolish enough to rush. But the energy in the room was sharper than the smoke. It was a static charge, a collective holding of breath by fifty of the finest minds in Europe.

James Adamson sat at a secondary terminal, wedged between a chain-smoking Italian engineer and a bank of blinking Tektronix oscilloscopes. His hands, usually steady when hovering over the keys of the VAX back in Cambridge, were trembling.

"Injection sequence initiating," a voice announced over the tannoy, speaking the flat, unaccented English of international science. "Protons are in the ring."

James watched the green phosphor screen in front of him. It wasn't showing the raw feed—that was a mess of noise for the hardware team. His screen was running a patch. A filter.

*The Adamson Metric.*

"You are sure the trigger logic is correct?" Henri whispered, leaning over James’s shoulder. The large Frenchman smelled of stale espresso and industrial soap. "If we tell the UA1 detector to ignore the standard scattering, and your ghost particle doesn't show up, we capture nothing. We waste a run. Rubbia will have my head, and then he will have yours."

"It’s not a ghost," James said, his voice sounding distant to his own ears. "And it’s not a particle. Not in the way you think."

"Just tell me the code is good, James."

James looked at the command line. `RUN_ADAMSON_FILTER.EXE`. The cursor blinked, a rhythmic heartbeat.

"The code is good," James said. "It’s the universe that’s messy. The code just straightens it out."

"Ramping up," the tannoy announced. "Energy approaching two-hundred-seventy GeV."

The floor beneath them vibrated. It was subtle, a subsonic thrum that traveled up through the soles of James’s trainers and settled in his teeth. Miles of underground magnets were singing in unison, bending a beam of protons moving at 99.9% the speed of light. Somewhere else in that 7-kilometer ring, a beam of antiprotons was spinning the other way.

James closed his eyes for a second. He pictured the layout he had drawn on a napkin in The Eagle pub months ago.

Standard physics said these beams were billions of particles, a swarm of marbles heading for a collision. But James didn't see a swarm. He saw a thread. One incredibly long, single thread, knotted into a complexity so dense it looked like solid matter. The electron—or the proton, or the quark—wasn't a distinct object. It was the same traveler, zipping back and forth through time, stitching the fabric of reality together one interaction at a time.

If he was right, the collision wouldn't just be an explosion. It would be a momentary tangle. A knot pulling tight.

"Beams are stable," the voice said. "Preparing for collisions in five... four..."

The chatter in the room died. The only sound was the high-pitched whine of the cooling fans and the *click-click-click* of a lighter somewhere in the back.

"Three... two... one. Collisions."

James’s eyes snapped open.

On the main wall, the massive displays lit up with the "spaghetti"—the chaotic spray of tracks from the particle smash. To the untrained eye, it was modern art. To the physicists in the room, it was data to be harvested, hunting for the heavy W and Z bosons that would win them a Nobel Prize.

But on James’s small screen, nothing happened.

The prompt blinked. `WAITING FOR EVENT SIGNATURE...`

"Nothing," Henri hissed, gripping the back of James’s chair. "James, the detector isn't triggering. We’re missing the luminosity."

"Wait," James said.

"We are blind! Switch off the filter!"

"No," James said, his hand covering the keyboard. "It’s there. It has to be there."

He thought of Eleanor back in the cold, damp lab at Cambridge. He thought of the noise in the radio telescope data, the glitch that refused to die. He thought of the sheer, terrifying loneliness of a universe built from a single point of consciousness playing solitaire with itself across eternity.

*Don't look at the crowd,* James thought, projecting the thought down into the tunnel. *Look at the face in the crowd.*

"James, I am overriding—" Henri reached for the control panel.

`EVENT DETECTED.`

The text flashed green.

"Stop!" James shouted.

Henri froze.

On the screen, the data began to parse. It didn't look like the spaghetti on the main wall. It didn't look like a scatter.

The vector lines drew themselves rapidly, glowing bright green against the black void of the monitor. They formed a perfect, closed loop. A figure-eight twisted on itself, defying the laws of momentum conservation—unless the particle had bounced off a wall of time that hadn't happened yet.

It was a signature. A fingerprint.

"Mon Dieu," Henri breathed. "Is that... is that a calibration error?"

"No," James whispered, the hairs on his arms standing up. "It’s an echo."

Around them, the room erupted. The main screens had picked up something anomalous—a massive energy spike that vanished as quickly as it appeared, leaving a hole in the standard model data where energy should have been.

"What was that?" someone shouted from the front row. "We lost the beam energy! Where did it go?"

James watched his screen. The loop rotated, a mathematical object of perfect beauty.

He knew where the energy went. It hadn't gone anywhere. It had simply gone *when*. It had folded back to 1981, to a glitch in a radio telescope. It had folded back to 1945, to a phone call between Wheeler and Feynman. It had gone forward, to a moment James hadn't lived yet.

The screen flashed a set of coordinates. Not spatial coordinates. Temporal displacement values.

James typed a query: `IDENTIFY SOURCE.`

The VAX churned for a second, the hard drive grinding.

`SOURCE: SELF.`

James sat back, the cheap plastic chair creaking under him. The roar of the control room—scientists shouting, printers clacking, the jubilant confusion of discovery—faded into a dull murmur.

He looked at the loop on the screen. It wasn't static. It was waving.

The single electron. The lonely traveler. It wasn't just building the universe. It was waiting for someone to notice the construction work. It was waiting for *him*.

Henri was slapping his back, laughing in a mix of hysteria and relief. "You see this, James? You see the missing mass? We have found something new! We have found a ghost!"

James smiled, but it was a tired, knowing smile. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a crumpled punch card he’d kept as a talisman from his first week at Cambridge.

"Not a ghost, Henri," James said softly.

He typed one final command to save the dataset to the tape reels.

`SAVE: EVENT_ONE`

James looked up at the main screen, where the chaotic tracks of the collision were frozen in amber. He realized with a jolt of vertigo that he wasn't looking at physics anymore. He was looking at a mirror.

"It's just us," James said to the room that wasn't listening. "It's all just us."

He watched the screen, knowing that billions of particles were just one traveler waving back at him across time. The paper he had written was just the prologue. The math was just the map.

James Adamson leaned forward, the green light of the monitor reflecting in his wide, unblinking eyes.

The adventure was just beginning.
