# Chapter 1: The Brown Envelope


The hallway carpet was a hideous, swirling vortex of paisley and brown, a geometric atrocity that James Adamson had memorized down to the last fiber.

He paced. Seven steps from the foot of the stairs to the front door. Turn. Seven steps back. Turn.

It was October 1981. Outside, the sky over rural Sussex was the color of a wet slate, threatening a rain that would turn the narrow country lanes into mulch. Inside, James was running a high-frequency loop, a subroutine of anxiety that was overheating his central processing unit.

*Seven steps.*

If the universe was indeed deterministic, as the classical physicists claimed, then the letter was already in the postman’s bag. The ink was dry. The decision had been made months ago in a wood-paneled room in Cambridge. The waveform had collapsed. He was either a student of Trinity College, or he was a failure. The binary nature of the situation was comforting in the abstract, but terrifying in the immediate execution.

*Turn. Seven steps.*

James stopped by the telephone table. Beside the rotary phone lay the latest issue of *Your Computer* and a cassette tape containing a program he’d spent three weeks writing on his Sinclair ZX81. It was a cellular automaton, a primitive attempt to simulate life using pixels that flickered on and off based on the state of their neighbors.

It had crashed the computer three times last night. The little black plastic wedge, with its membrane keyboard and wobbly 16K RAM pack, simply couldn't handle the complexity.

"James, if you pace any more, you'll wear through the underlay," his mother’s voice drifted from the kitchen, accompanied by the clatter of a kettle.

"I’m checking the structural integrity of the floorboards, Mum," James muttered, though loud enough for her to hear.

"Check the kettle instead. Make yourself a tea. A watched pot never boils, and a watched letterbox never... whatever it does."

James ignored the tea. He stared at the frosted glass of the front door. He wasn't just waiting for a letter; he was waiting for permission to exist. In the sleepy village of Oakhaven, James was an anomaly. While his peers were worrying about learning to drive Ford Cortinas or who was playing on *Top of the Pops*, James was lying awake at 3:00 AM worrying about the heat death of the universe. He was a glitch in the village's source code.

He needed Cambridge. He needed the stone walls, the damp courts, the ghosts of Newton and Maxwell. He needed to be where the hardware was powerful enough to run the software in his head.

A sound.

James froze. It was the distinct, high-torque whine of a Bedford HA van. The Royal Mail.

He pressed his face against the cold glass, distorting his reflection. Through the pebbled surface, the world outside looked pixelated, low-resolution. The red blur of the van pulled up to the curb.

James held his breath. This was it. The Schrödinger moment. Until the letter fell through the slot, he was both accepted and rejected. He was in a superposition of states.

The figure in the navy uniform walked up the path. James backed away, retreating to the stairs. He couldn't be seen standing there like a desperate spaniel. It wasn't dignified. It wasn't *scientific*.

*Clack.*

The brass flap lifted. A single object slid through and landed on the doormat with a heavy, authoritative *thud*.

It wasn't white. It wasn't a bill. It was a brown A4 envelope. Thick. Heavy.

James stared at it. In the hierarchy of academic correspondence, thickness was everything. Rejection letters were thin, polite, and devastatingly light. They contained a single sheet of paper saying, *“We regret to inform you...”*

Acceptance letters were heavy. They contained forms. Accommodation details. Reading lists. The admin of a new life.

He walked the seven steps. His heart was hammering a rhythm that felt dangerously syncopated. He picked it up.

*University of Cambridge.*
*Trinity College.*
*Office of Admissions.*

He ran his thumb over the seal. For a second, his mind drifted to a documentary he’d watched on the BBC about the double-slit experiment. When you observed the electron, it chose a path. By opening this, he was forcing the universe to decide what kind of story this was going to be.

He ripped the corner.

He pulled out the sheaf of papers. The first page was cream-colored, watermarked, stiff with tradition.

*Dear Mr. Adamson,*

*I am pleased to inform you that you have been accepted to read Natural Sciences...*

The breath rushed out of him, a deflation of tension so sudden he had to grab the banister. The hallway stopped spinning. The paisley carpet suddenly looked less like a vortex and more like... just a carpet.

"James?" his mother called out, the tone sharper this time. She sensed the silence.

"It's brown," James croaked. Then, louder. "Mum, it's the brown envelope."

She appeared in the kitchen doorway, drying her hands on a tea towel. She looked at his face, pale but grinning maniacally, and then at the thick bundle of papers in his hand. She smiled, a warm, relieved expression that softened the worry lines around her eyes.

"Well," she said, nodding at the bundle. "I suppose we’ll need to buy you a gown then. And a warmer coat."

James looked back down at the letter. He wasn't thinking about coats. He was thinking about the date. October 1982 entry. He had a year. A gap year to work, to read, to prepare. But the destination was locked in.

He looked at the dust motes dancing in the shaft of grey light coming through the letterbox. There were thousands of them, swirling in the air current.

A thought struck him, unbidden and strange. It was something he had read in a library book about Richard Feynman, a half-remembered joke about telephones and time travel.

*What if it’s just the same dust mote?* James thought, watching a speck vanish into the shadow. *What if it’s just one particle moving so fast it creates the illusion of a cloud?*

He shook his head, dismissing the thought as the adrenaline of the moment. He was going to Cambridge. He was going to the place where they split the atom.

James Adamson clutched the envelope to his chest. The simulation had leveled him up. The tutorial was over.

"Tea," James said, finally stepping away from the door. "I think I will have that tea now."

"Milk and two sugars," his mother said, heading back to the kettle. "You're going to need the energy. I hear they work you to death up there."

James followed her, the thick envelope tucked under his arm like a blast shield. He had no idea that the work hadn't even started yet. He had no idea that the universe he was so eager to study was already fraying at the edges, waiting for someone with the right eyes to pull the loose thread.

But for now, the variable was set to *TRUE*. The program continued.
