# Chapter 12: The Winter of Discontent


The adrenaline of the rebellion had a half-life, and James Adamson calculated it to be roughly six hours.

The defiant stride toward the computer lab at the end of the departmental review had felt heroic. It felt like Galileo muttering *“And yet it moves”* under his breath. But Galileo had been under house arrest in a Tuscan villa with decent wine. James was in a damp student rental on Jesus Lane, and the only thing moving was the mold creeping up the wallpaper.

By the time December settled over Cambridge, the manic heat of his resolve had been leeched away by the fog. The Fenland winter didn't just chill you; it desaturated you. The sky was a persistent, monolithic slab of white-grey, pressing down on the spires of the university like the lid of a Tupperware container.

James sat on the edge of his bed, staring at the stack of papers on his desk. The draft. The Adamson Metric.

Three days ago, those equations had looked like the sheet music of the cosmos. Today, in the flat, watery light of a Tuesday afternoon, they looked like what Professor Halloway’s colleagues had called them: *Numerology. Pattern-seeking behavior. A breakdown.*

He hadn’t mimeographed the paper. He hadn’t sent it to the radicals. He hadn’t done anything except lie in bed, listening to the rain drum against the single-pane glass, engaging in a terrified staring contest with the nature of reality.

The problem with the Single Electron Theory wasn’t the math. The math was elegant. The problem was the philosophy.

If Feynman and Wheeler were right—if James was right—then the universe wasn’t a vast, populated place. It was an empty room containing one frantic, lonely actor changing costumes at the speed of light.

James looked at his hand, flexing the fingers. Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen. Electrons spinning in shells.

If the theory held, the electrons in his fingertips were the exact same entity as the electrons in the peeling paint on the windowsill. The same electron that was currently powering the streetlamp outside. The same electron that had comprised the brain of Albert Einstein and the beak of a dodo.

It was a solipsistic nightmare. A cosmic horror story disguised as particle physics.

*I am alone,* he thought, the concept heavy in his chest. *Not just socially alone. Ontologically alone. The universe is just Me, interacting with Myself, across an infinite expanse of time, trying to pretend I’m not the only thing that exists.*

He stood up, grabbing his heavy wool coat. The silence of the room was screaming at him. He needed noise. He needed the illusion of multiplicity.

***

Cambridge at Christmas was a ghost town of stone and locked gates. The undergraduates had fled to the warmth of their parents' estates or the chaotic joy of family holidays. The streets were abandoned to the townies, the tourists brave enough to weather the drizzle, and the stragglers like James who couldn't bear to take their failures home.

He walked past the closed shops of King’s Parade. A radio in an electronics store window was barking out the synthesized, robotic beat of *The Human League*.

*“Don’t, don’t you want me? You know I can’t believe it when I hear that you won’t see me…”*

The synthesizer riff was cold, precise, and digital. It fit the mood of 1982 perfectly. The world was shedding the fuzzy, warm analog skin of the 70s and hardening into something jagged and neon.

James found himself drifting toward the only place where the deterministic universe felt safe: the arcade.

It was a dim cavern smelling of stale cigarette smoke and ozone. He fed a ten-pence piece into a *Tempest* machine. The vector graphics spun—clean, sharp lines of pure geometry glowing against the void.

He played reflexively, his hands moving without conscious thought. He wasn't shooting aliens; he was manipulating variables.

*Zap. Zap. Zap.*

“You’re going to die,” a voice said.

James jumped, losing a life as his claw-ship shattered into pixelated dust. He looked down. A local kid, maybe twelve years old, in a parka three sizes too big, was watching him.

“I know,” James muttered. “It’s inevitable. Entropy wins.”

“No, I mean you’re trapped in the corner,” the kid said, wiping his nose on his sleeve. “You gotta use the Superzapper.”

James looked at the screen. “I’m saving it.”

“For what? The game ends when you run out of lives, mate. You can’t take it with you.”

James stared at the glowing vector web. The kid was right. A profound, stupidly simple truth. He was hoarding potential energy in a system designed to drain it.

He walked away from the machine, leaving the last life unplayed.

***

He ended up at the computer lab. He shouldn't have had access—the building was officially closed for the break—but Eleanor had shown him how to shimmy the lock on the side door near the bike sheds, and the porters were all likely drunk on sherry by now.

The VAX-11/780 hummed in the darkness, the only warm thing in the building. Its blinking lights were a comforting, rhythmic heartbeat. This machine didn't care about tenure, or departmental politics, or the distinct lack of 'realism' in his theories. It only cared about logic. *If This, Then That.*

James sat at the terminal, the green cursor blinking at him.

`> LOGIN: J_ADAMSON`
`> PASSWORD: ********`

He navigated to the directory containing his life’s work. `METRIC_V4.DAT`.

He had been so sure. The Adamson Metric predicted that the electron wasn't jumping randomly; it was traversing a specific, recursive path, a knot in spacetime that looked chaotic only because human perception was limited to three spatial dimensions. He had derived a formula to predict the 'next' appearance of the particle.

But the board had laughed.

*“Mr. Adamson,”* the Chair had said, peering over his spectacles, *“you have rewritten Zeno’s Paradox and called it astrophysics. This is not science. It is a desperate attempt to find order where there is only probability. We suggest you take the holidays to reconsider your trajectory at this institution.”*

Reconsider his trajectory. That was polite academic speak for *quit before we kick you out.*

James typed a command.

`> DELETE METRIC_V4.DAT`

The cursor blinked. It waited for confirmation.

`> ARE YOU SURE? (Y/N)`

His finger hovered over the 'Y'.

It would be so easy. He could pivot. He was smart; he could do something boring and respectable. He could study fluid dynamics. He could model weather patterns. He could get a job in London at a bank, calculating interest rates until his soul calcified. He could stop being the crazy kid who thought the universe was a single thread.

He looked around the empty lab. The shadows stretched long across the linoleum floor.

He thought about the static in the radio telescope data. The glitch. The pattern that shouldn't be there.

If he deleted this, he was admitting the universe was random. He was admitting that chaos ruled. He was admitting that he was just a biological accident on a wet rock, circling a dying star, with no connection to the past or the future.

But if he pressed 'N'...

If he pressed 'N', he was committing to the madness. He was accepting the burden of the One Electron. He was accepting that he was effectively alone in a universe of his own making, trapped in an infinite loop of time.

The cursor blinked.

*On. Off. On. Off.*

Existence. Non-existence.

The silence of the lab was heavy, oppressive. The winter of 1982 lay thick against the windows, a world of grey rain and cold concrete. He felt small. He felt stupid.

"I'm tired," he whispered to the VAX.

He reached out. His finger trembled.

He didn't press 'Y'. He didn't press 'N'.

James Adamson turned off the monitor.

The green glow faded, plunging him into darkness. He left the question unanswered, the file suspended in magnetic purgatory. He pulled his coat tight around his neck and walked out into the rain, leaving the computer humming in the dark, holding the secret he was too afraid to keep and too terrified to destroy.

He would decide tomorrow. Or the day after. For now, he would just exist in the static.
