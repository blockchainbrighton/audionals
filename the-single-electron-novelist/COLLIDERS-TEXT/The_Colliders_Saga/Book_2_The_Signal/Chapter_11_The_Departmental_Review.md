# Chapter 11: The Departmental Review


The seminar room in the Cavendish Laboratory smelled of floor wax, damp wool, and the terrifying, accumulated intellect of three hundred years of physics. It was a smell that usually inspired James. Today, it made him want to vomit.

He stood before the blackboard, a stick of white chalk sweating in his grip. Behind him, the dust of his equations swirled in the shafts of afternoon light that pierced the high windows.

"So," said Dr. Aris Thorne, leaning back in his chair. The wood creaked—a gunshot in the silence. Thorne was the department’s heavy hitter in Quantum Chromodynamics, a man who looked at the universe and saw only sticky gluons and rigid rules. "If I understand this... *salad* of geometry correctly, Mr. Adamson, you are suggesting that the positron we observed yesterday in the cloud chamber is the same electron that powered the lightbulb in this room ten minutes ago?"

James swallowed. "Not just the same type, sir. The same entity. The exact same particle, taking a different path through the temporal dimension."

"And the billions of electrons currently constituting your own nervous system?" Thorne asked, his eyebrows raising.

"One electron," James said, his voice trembling but gaining traction. "Moving backward and forward through time so rapidly, weaving such a complex knot, that a cross-section of that knot at any given moment—our 'now'—appears to be countless individual particles. Like slicing through a ball of yarn and seeing a hundred points of wool, when in reality, it is one continuous string."

He turned back to the board. He had drawn a crude diagram of the Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory, but he had overlaid it with his own discovery: *The Adamson Metric.*

"The issue with Wheeler’s original proposal," James said, speaking faster now, the mania of the last three sleepless nights kicking in, "was the mass imbalance. There are too many electrons and not enough positrons. The math didn't hold. But that’s because we were assuming a chaotic universe."

James tapped the board hard enough to snap the chalk.

"We assume the universe has infinite memory," he said. "We assume it calculates every interaction from scratch. But the data from the radio telescope—the glitches I found—suggests optimization. The universe doesn't store the position of $10^{80}$ particles. It stores the trajectory of *one* and renders the rest."

He spun around to face the panel. There were three of them. Dr. Thorne, looking bored; Professor Halloway, looking deeply pained and staring at his shoes; and old Dr. Abernathy, who appeared to be asleep.

"It’s a rendering artifact," James insisted. "The mass imbalance is a buffering error. The universe is a computation, and I have found the clock speed."

Silence. Absolute, suffocating silence.

Dr. Thorne took off his glasses and cleaned them with a handkerchief. "Mr. Adamson, are you familiar with the term 'Apophenia'?"

James blinked. "Pattern recognition, sir?"

"The tendency to perceive meaningful connections between unrelated things," Thorne corrected coldly. "Seeing faces in the clouds. Or, in your case, seeing a computer program in background cosmic radiation."

"The math works," James pleaded. He gestured to the sheaf of papers on the table. "I ran the simulation on the VAX. The recursion holds. If you apply the Metric, the noise in the data vanishes. It becomes a signal."

"You have fitted a curve to noise, James," Halloway said softly, finally looking up. His eyes were full of pity, which was worse than Thorne’s scorn. "It’s a classic error. You wanted to find a pattern, so you built a mathematical framework that forced one to appear. It is elegant, certainly. But it is not physics."

"It predicts," James said, desperate now. He grabbed the last page of his report. "Look at page twelve. I’ve calculated the trajectory for the next run at the Super Proton Synchrotron in Geneva. Not a probability cloud. An exact coordinate. Time and place. If the theory is wrong, the particle won't be there. If I'm right, it has to be."

Thorne picked up the paper, glanced at the prediction, and dropped it back onto the table as if it were a used tissue.

"Geneva is hunting for the W boson, Mr. Adamson. Serious men are doing serious work to validate the Standard Model. They are not looking for... what was your phrase? 'A retro-causal echo of a single cosmic thread.'" Thorne sighed. "This is numerology dressed up as astrophysics. It is mysticism with a bit of calculus thrown on top to make it palatable."

"It’s hard science!" James snapped, his control fraying. "Feynman proposed the One-Electron universe in the forties! Was he a mystic?"

"Feynman knew when he was joking," Thorne said, his voice hardening. "And he knew that when the math didn't fit observation, you threw it out. You didn't rewrite the fundamental nature of reality to accommodate a glitch in a radio telescope."

Old Dr. Abernathy stirred, his eyes snapping open. He looked at the blackboard, then at James.

"Is this the boy with the video games?" Abernathy wheezed.

"Yes," Halloway muttered.

"Thought so," Abernathy grunted. "Too much time looking at screens. Not enough time looking at the stars. The universe is not a Pac-Man machine, son. It is messy. It is stochastic. It does not run on a clock."

"But what if it does?" James whispered. "What if the mess is just the compression algorithm?"

Thorne stood up. The review was over.

"James," Thorne said, and he used the first name with a patronizing weight that crushed the air out of James’s lungs. "You are a bright student. You have a gift for calculation. But you are drifting. This paper? It belongs in a science fiction magazine, not the Cavendish archives. We will not be publishing this. And for your own reputation, I suggest you do not circulate it further."

"But the prediction—"

"Forget the prediction," Thorne snapped. "Focus on your actual coursework. Analyze the spectral lines of Alpha Centauri like you were asked. Leave the 'Source Code of Creation' to the theologians."

Thorne gathered his things and walked out without a backward glance. Abernathy followed, shaking his head and muttering about the decline of standards since the war.

Only Halloway remained. He sat at the table, rubbing his temples.

James stood by the blackboard, the white dust on his hands feeling like ash. The equation for the Adamson Metric loomed behind him—$A_{\mu\nu} = \partial_{\mu}\phi \dots$—a beautiful, symmetry-breaking truth that no one wanted to look at.

"I'm right, Professor," James said, his voice hollow. "I know I'm right."

Halloway sighed, standing up and buttoning his tweed jacket. "Being right isn't enough, James. You have to be right in a way they can understand. You've scared them. You’ve walked in here and told the high priests that their god is just a faulty circuit board."

"It's the truth."

"It's lonely," Halloway said. He walked to the door, then paused. "Burn the paper, James. Or at least, hide it until you have tenure. They’ll eat you alive if you try to push this now."

Then he was gone.

James stood alone in the lecture theatre. The silence rushed back in, heavier than before. He looked at the paper on the table—the one Thorne had dropped.

*Target: SPS, Geneva. Run 44-Alpha.*

The data didn't exist yet. The experiment hadn't happened. The collision was scheduled for May.

He picked up the paper and folded it into his pocket. His hands were shaking, not from fear anymore, but from a cold, crystallizing anger. They called it numerology. They called it a game.

He walked over to the blackboard. He didn't erase the equation. He didn't wipe away the diagram of the single electron knotting itself through the fabric of history. He left it there, a graffito in the temple of the blind.

James walked out of the Cavendish Laboratory and into the biting Cambridge wind. The sky was a flat, oppressive grey, the concrete of the campus slick with drizzle. It was 1982, and the world was cold, analogue, and stupid.

He pulled his coat tight. He wouldn't burn the paper. He would mimeograph it. He would send it to every crackpot, every radical, and every outcast in the physics community.

If the high priests wouldn't listen, he’d preach to the choir.

He turned toward the computer lab, his stride lengthening. The winter was coming, but James Adamson was just heating up.
