# Chapter 2: Gowns and Space Invaders


Cambridge in October of 1982 did not look like a university; it looked like a Gothic theme park designed to intimidate teenagers from the countryside.

James Adamson stood before the Great Gate of Trinity College, a battered leather suitcase in one hand and a map that seemed to have been drawn by a drunk cartographer in the other. Above him, the stone statue of Henry VIII held a table leg instead of a sceptre—a centuries-old prank that no one had bothered to correct. That was the first lesson of Cambridge: tradition was just a mistake that everyone had agreed to keep making.

He dragged his suitcase over the cobblestones. The wheels complained with a rhythmic *thud-squeak, thud-squeak* that echoed off the high limestone walls. The air smelled of wet leaves, bicycle grease, and the distinct, musty odor of wealth.

James felt entirely too modern for this place, and yet, paradoxically, not modern enough. He was wearing denim and a parka; everyone else seemed to be floating around in sub-fusc and billowing black gowns, looking like low-budget vampires.

"Name?"

The Porter’s Lodge was a glass-walled fishbowl occupied by a man in a bowler hat who looked like he had personally denied entry to Napoleon.

"Adamson," James said, his voice cracking slightly. "James. First year. Astrophysics."

The Porter ran a finger down a ledger that looked older than the American Constitution. "Adamson. Staircase P. Room 4. You’re late, Mr. Adamson. The Master’s reception has already begun. I suggest you don your gown."

"I... I haven't got a gown yet."

The Porter peered over his spectacles. The look suggested that James had just admitted to not having a soul. "Town tailors are closed. You’ll have to borrow a spare from the linen closet. Staircase P. Top floor. Don't trip."

***

An hour later, James was drowning in black polyester.

He sat in a dining hall that looked suspiciously like a cathedral, surrounded by three hundred other nervous eighteen-year-olds. The room was lit by candles, despite the fact that electricity had been common usage for decades. The shadows danced high up in the hammer-beam roof, hiding the faces of portraits of men who had discovered elements and invented calculus.

James felt the crushing weight of Imposter Syndrome settling on his chest like a damp wool blanket. The guy to his left, a blond boy with a jawline sharp enough to cut glass, was talking loudly about his summer rowing in Henley. The girl to his right was discussing the nuances of 14th-century French poetry.

James stared at his soup. He was thinking about the viscosity of the liquid and how the heat transfer was creating miniature convection currents, little galaxies of cream swirling in a universe of tomato.

"Pass the salt, would you?" The rower nudged him.

James handed it over. "It’s sodium chloride," he muttered.

"Sorry?"

"Nothing."

It was a performance. All of it. The gowns, the Latin grace, the high table where the Fellows sat like gods on Olympus eating better food than the mortals below. It was a render. A high-resolution graphical interface designed to hide the fact that they were all just hairless apes trying to figure out how to use tools.

He needed to get out. He needed air. Or better yet, he needed logic.

***

Professor Halloway’s office was located in a turret that James was fairly certain violated several fire codes. To get there, one had to ascend a spiral staircase that seemed designed to weed out the physically unfit before they could submit a thesis.

James knocked on the heavy oak door two days later, his tutorial essay clutched in a sweaty hand.

"Enter if you must!" a voice bellowed.

James pushed the door open. The room was a chaotic explosion of paper. Stacks of journals formed stalagmites on the floor. A blackboard covered one entire wall, dusted with a fine layer of chalk and scribbled with equations that looked like the frantic scrawlings of a madman.

Professor Halloway sat behind a desk that was barely visible beneath the debris. He was a man composed entirely of angles and tweed, with hair that suggested he had recently been electrocuted.

"Adamson," Halloway said, not looking up from a chart he was dissecting with a red pen. "The boy from the sticks. High marks in mathematics, terrible interview skills."

"Thank you, sir," James said, unsure if it was a compliment.

"I’ve read your preliminary essay on stellar nucleosynthesis." Halloway finally looked up. His eyes were piercing, magnified by thick glasses. "It’s boring."

James blinked. "Sir? The math is correct."

"The math is *competent*," Halloway corrected, standing up and brushing ash from his waistcoat. "Competence is the enemy of brilliance, Adamson. You treat the universe like a clockwork toy. Tick, tock. Star burns, star explodes, carbon forms, life happens. It’s too... tidy."

Halloway walked to the window, looking out over the manicured lawn of the Great Court.

"Physics isn't about tidiness," Halloway said, his back to James. "It's about the mess. It's about the things that don't fit. The math is just the language we use to beg the universe to make sense. But the universe doesn't care about your math. It is chaotic, violent, and fundamentally absurd."

He turned back, pointing a chalk-stained finger at James. "You are looking for rules, Mr. Adamson. I want you to look for the exceptions. Now, get out. And find a better barber. You look like a dandelion."

***

James didn't go to the library. The library was full of answers, and Halloway had just told him that answers were boring.

Instead, he walked. He walked out of the college gates, past the tourist traps selling postcards of the King's College Chapel, and down toward the grittier end of town. The ancient stone gave way to concrete and brick. The smell of old books was replaced by the smell of diesel and frying grease.

He heard it before he saw it. A synthesized, electronic pulse. A digital heartbeat.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

He ducked into a doorway under a flickering neon sign that simply read **ARCADE**.

It was a sensory assault. The air was thick with cigarette smoke and the ozone smell of overheating circuit boards. It was dark, illuminated only by the cathode-ray glow of two dozen cabinets lining the walls. This was the cathedral of the new age. No Latin here. Just 8-bit sound chips and the click-clack of microswitches.

James exchanged a pound note for a handful of silver 10p coins. He bypassed *Pac-Man*—too circular, too biological—and found an unoccupied *Space Invaders* cabinet in the corner.

He slotted the coin. The screen flickered.

*INSERT COIN* became *CREDIT 01*.

He pressed start. The green bunkers appeared. The formation of aliens descended.

James played with a cold, mechanical efficiency. He didn't panic when the shots rained down. He calculated. Left, shoot, right, wait. The laser blast traveled at a fixed velocity. The enemies moved in a deterministic grid.

*Drop. Reverse direction. Speed increase.*

As he fell into the trance, the world outside—the gowns, the sherry, Halloway’s criticism—faded away. There was only the loop.

And then, he saw it.

It wasn't a hallucination; it was a shift in perspective. He watched the alien sprite on the far left. It was identical to the alien on the far right. Not similar—*identical*. It used the same memory address. The computer wasn't generating fifty unique aliens. It was rendering one alien, fifty times, in different coordinate locations.

James froze, his finger hovering over the fire button. A pixelated bomb hit his tank. *GAME OVER*.

He stared at the "Game Over" screen, the letters pulsing in the darkness.

"It's just a refresh rate," he whispered to the smoke-filled room.

If you had a fast enough processor, you wouldn't need a billion electrons to build a universe. You would only need one. One electron, whizzing back and forth through time and space so fast that it appeared to be everywhere at once. Like a sprite being drawn on a CRT monitor, line by line, so quickly the human eye sees a solid image.

The arcade machine was a closed system. A deterministic box. But what was the universe if not a box with a higher resolution?

"Hey, mate. You playing or staring?"

James snapped out of it. A kid with a spiked mohawk was waiting behind him.

"It's... it's all just reusing the assets," James mumbled, his mind racing.

"Right. Whatever, Einstein. Move over."

James stepped back, clutching his remaining coins. His heart was hammering against his ribs, not from the adrenaline of the game, but from the terrifying elegance of the thought. Halloway wanted him to look for the mess? Fine. But what if the mess was just a symptom of a system running out of memory?

He walked back out into the Cambridge night. The stars were out, piercing the grey cloud cover. For the first time, they didn't look like nuclear furnaces to him. They looked like pixels on a screen that was struggling to render the darkness.

He needed a computer. Not a cabinet in a smoky arcade, but a real machine. He needed to see the code.

James pulled his parka tight against the chill and started running back toward the college. The gowns could keep their traditions. James Adamson had just found the edge of the screen.
