# Chapter 3: The VAX-11/780


The Computer Laboratory, located in the stark, functional architecture of the Corn Exchange building, smelled of ozone, floor wax, and the nervous sweat of a hundred mathematics undergraduates. It was a sensory collision that James Adamson found instantly intoxicating.

If Trinity College was a temple to the past, built of limestone and Latin, this room was the cathedral of the future. The air conditioning hummed with a violent, industrial zeal, necessary to keep the deity cool.

And there it was. The VAX-11/780.

It wasn't as visually impressive as the sci-fi paperbacks promised. It looked like a series of beige and purple washing machines bolted together, dominating the far wall behind a glass partition. But James knew better. Inside those cabinets, silicon logic gates were snapping open and shut at speeds the human brain couldn't comprehend. It was a deterministic beast. Input went in; truth came out. There was no room for interpretation, no room for poetry, and—crucially—no room for ghosts.

James, however, was currently wrestling with the analogue interface to this digital god: a stack of punch cards.

"Damn it," he hissed, his fingers fumbling.

He was standing at the card reader, a mechanical mouth that swallowed cardboard and spat out data. He had spent the last six hours punching Hollerith code for a simple orbital mechanics simulation—a translation of Kepler’s laws into FORTRAN. He had the physics in his head, but his hands were betraying him.

The stack slipped.

It was physics in action: gravity acting upon mass, friction failing to compensate. Two hundred cards cascaded out of his hands, fluttering like dead leaves, scattering across the linoleum floor.

"Oh, marvelous," James muttered, dropping to his knees. "Entropy wins again."

"You didn't number them."

The voice came from above. It was cool, clipped, and devoid of sympathy.

James looked up. A girl was leaning against a desk nearby, watching him with the detached curiosity of a biologist observing a struggling beetle. She had sharp features framed by a severe bob cut, and she wore an oversized woolen jumper that made her look like a frantic creative, though her eyes were pure engineer.

"I beg your pardon?" James said, scrambling to gather the cards.

"The cards," she said, pointing a slender finger. "You didn't write sequence numbers on the back. If you had, you could just sort them. Now, you have to re-read the code on every single one to put them back in order. See you in three hours."

James looked at the chaotic spread of cardboard. She was right. He slumped back on his heels, defeated. "I'm used to calculating with a pencil. Pencil lead doesn't scatter."

"Pencils are slow. The VAX is fast. The VAX doesn't care if you're clumsy, only if you're logical." She turned back to her station, a glowing green VT100 video terminal. "Leave the cardboard. Grab a terminal. It’s 1982, for God’s sake. Direct entry is the only way to breathe."

James abandoned the pile. He felt foolish, a relic of the 1970s already. He pulled up a chair next to her. The green phosphor cursor on her screen blinked with a hypnotic rhythm. *Blink. Blink. Blink.*

"I'm James," he offered. "Trinity. Astrophysics."

"Eleanor," she replied, not looking away from her screen. Her fingers flew across the keyboard, producing a cascade of parentheses and commands that looked nothing like the FORTRAN James had been studying. "King’s. Mathematics and Computer Science."

James squinted at her monitor. "That’s not FORTRAN."

"LISP," Eleanor said. "List Processing. It’s for symbol manipulation. Artificial Intelligence."

James laughed, a short, sharp sound. "AI? You mean like HAL 9000? You're trying to teach that purple refrigerator to think?"

Eleanor stopped typing. She swiveled her chair to face him, her expression intensifying. "I'm trying to teach it to learn. There's a difference. You physicists, you look at the universe and see billiard balls banging into each other. Deterministic. Boring."

"The universe *is* billiard balls," James countered, feeling the residual adrenaline from his arcade epiphany. "It's all trajectories. If you know the position and momentum of every particle, you can predict the future. It’s just a massive computation."

"Laplace's Demon," Eleanor nodded, recognizing the theory. "But the Demon can't exist because of Heisenberg. You can't know position and momentum simultaneously. Your universe is fuzzy, James. Mine is binary. True or False. One or Zero."

"But can it think?" James gestured to the mainframe behind the glass. "Can it actually understand what you're feeding it?"

"Does it matter?" Eleanor tapped her temple. "What happens in here? Neurons fire. Electrical potential moves across a synapse. It’s a signal. On or off. If I build a network complex enough, with enough nodes, enough recursive loops... who is to say it’s not thinking? Is your brain magic, James? Or is it just wetware running a script?"

James stared at the cursor blinking on the empty screen of his terminal. The concept hit him with the force of a physical blow.

*Wetware running a script.*

He thought back to the Space Invaders cabinet. The aliens moved because the code told them to. They died because the collision detection logic returned a 'true' value.

"If intelligence is just math," James murmured, half to himself, "and the universe is just physics... and physics is just math..."

"Then everything is computation," Eleanor finished for him. She smiled, a quick, predatory flash of teeth. "Welcome to the real science. Now, are you going to stare at the void, or are you going to log in?"

James turned to his keyboard. He felt a strange vibration in his fingertips. He wasn't just typing; he was interfacing.

"Show me," James said. "Show me how to bypass the cards."

For the next two hours, the rivalry dissolved into a tutorial. Eleanor was a harsh instructor, intolerant of syntax errors, but she was brilliant. She showed him how to invoke the editor, how to compile directly on the mainframe, how to view his errors in real-time.

As James typed, translating his orbital equations into lines of code, the VAX-11/780 ceased to be a machine. It became a telescope.

"You're trying to model a three-body problem?" Eleanor asked, peering over his shoulder as lines of code scrolled up his screen.

"Simpler," James said. "I'm looking at data density. I'm trying to figure out why the universe wastes so much space. Why is there so much empty vacuum between the interesting bits? It’s inefficient."

"Maybe it's memory management," Eleanor cracked. "The system can't render atoms everywhere, so it uses 'void' as a placeholder to save RAM."

She was joking, but James didn't laugh. His fingers hovered over the 'RETURN' key.

*Memory management.*

"Eleanor," James said slowly. "If you were writing a program... a simulation... and you had limited memory, how would you handle an electron?"

"An object class," she said instantly. "Define the properties: charge, mass, spin. Then you instantiate it whenever you need it."

"But would you make a trillion unique electrons? Or would you just make one definition and project it a trillion times?"

Eleanor frowned, processing the abstract logic. "If I wanted to save memory? I'd use a pointer. One instance of 'Electron' in the memory, and just reference it wherever it needs to appear on the screen. Why?"

James pressed 'RETURN'. The screen flickered as the VAX compiled his clumsy code.

"Just thinking," James said. "About efficiency."

The compiler spat out a string of errors. *FATAL ERROR: RECURSION LIMIT EXCEEDED.*

"You broke it," Eleanor noted dryly. " infinite loop."

"No," James stared at the glowing text. "I didn't break it. I just asked it a question it couldn't answer yet."

He looked past the terminal, through the glass partition, at the hulking VAX mainframe. The lights on the front panel were dancing—a frantic strobe of activity. Somewhere in those silicon chips, electrons were racing through gates, mimicking the logic of the cosmos.

He had come here to escape the fuzzy uncertainty of philosophy, but he had found something far more disturbing. He had found a language. And for the first time, he suspected that the universe might actually speak it.

"I need to learn more," James said, standing up. The punch cards on the floor were forgotten trash. "I need to know how the machine thinks."

Eleanor looked up at him, the glow of the green screen illuminating her face like a digital specter. "Careful, James. Stare at the code long enough, and you stop seeing the stars. You only see the pixels."

"Maybe," James said, grabbing his coat. "Or maybe I'll finally see what makes the pixels shine."

He walked out of the lab, leaving the hum of the cooling fans behind him. Outside, the Cambridge mist had rolled in, obscuring the stone gargoyles of the old buildings. The world looked grey, low-resolution.

James Adamson smiled. He wasn't afraid of the grey anymore. He was beginning to understand that the grey was just static. And static could be filtered.

He needed data. He needed the radio telescope logs Professor Halloway had mentioned. He wasn't just going to analyze them; he was going to debug them.
