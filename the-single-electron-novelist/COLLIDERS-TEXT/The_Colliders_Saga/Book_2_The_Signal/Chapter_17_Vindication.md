# Chapter 17: Vindication


The rain in Cambridge didn’t wash things clean; it just made the ancient stone smell of wet wool and centuries of accumulated damp. James Adamson stood outside the heavy oak door of Professor Halloway’s office, the humidity clinging to his hair.

He checked his watch. 9:00 AM.

He felt like a man walking to the gallows. Since Eleanor had dragged him out of the computer lab, he had been living in a state of suspended animation. The "Underground Paper" was circulating, yes, but so were the rumors of his imminent expulsion for misuse of university resources. He had hijacked the mainframe to prove that the universe was a single, recursive loop. The academic board usually preferred students who stuck to calculating star densities.

James knocked. The sound was hollow, swallowed by the wood.

"Enter."

The voice wasn’t the booming baritone James was used to. It was thin, brittle.

James pushed the door open. The office was exactly as it always was: a chaotic ecosystem of stacked journals, overflowing ashtrays, and a blackboard permanently dusted with the ghosts of equations past. But Professor Halloway was different.

The older man wasn’t sitting behind his desk, pontificating. He was standing by the window, staring out at the grey slate roofs of Trinity College. He didn’t turn around when the door latched shut.

"Sit down, James."

James sat. The leather chair creaked, a sound like a gunshot in the silence. He gripped his knees, preparing his defense. He would talk about the efficiency of the algorithm. He would mention Eleanor’s AI. He would beg, if necessary.

"I received a communication this morning," Halloway said, still addressing the window. "From a colleague. A frantic colleague, I might add."

"Professor, I can explain the mainframe usage," James started, the words tumbling out fast. "I know the CPU time was excessive, but the recursion needed—"

"Be quiet, James."

Halloway turned around.

James stopped breathing. The Professor looked… haunted. His usually ruddy complexion was the color of old parchment. His glasses hung low on his nose, and his hands, usually so steady when lighting his pipe, were trembling.

In his right hand, he held a strip of yellow telex paper. It was long, curling at the bottom, covered in the blocky, dot-matrix punch of a teleprinter.

"Do you know what is happening in Geneva right now?" Halloway asked, his voice barely a whisper.

James swallowed. "The Super Proton Synchrotron. They’re ramping up. Looking for the W boson."

"They are running the UA1 experiment," Halloway corrected, walking slowly toward the desk. He moved like he was carrying a heavy weight. "Smashing protons and antiprotons at 540 GeV. Trying to break the furniture to see how the wood is put together."

Halloway placed the yellow paper on the desk. He smoothed it out with a shaking palm.

"Three days ago, you distributed a mimeograph. A piece of… 'science fiction,' as Dean Miller called it. In it, you utilized your 'Adamson Metric' to predict a specific anomaly in the background noise of a high-energy collision. You claimed that because the electron is a single entity moving through time, at high enough energies, we should see a 'echo' of the particle interacting with its own future state."

James nodded, his throat dry. "The Retro-Causal Echo. Yes."

"You assigned a value to this echo," Halloway said. He looked down at the telex, then up at James. "You predicted the missing transverse energy would not be random. You gave a specific coordinate for the resonance."

"Yes."

"Do you remember the number, James?"

"4.119283001 GeV," James recited instantly. It was burned into his retinas. It was the number the universe had whispered to him in the static.

Halloway stared at him for a long, agonizing second. Then, he slid the telex across the mahogany desk.

"Read the third line."

James picked up the paper. The text was jagged, printed by a machine in a rush.

**FROM: CERN / SPS CONTROL / GENEVA**
**TO: DEPT ASTROPHYSICS / CAMBRIDGE**
**ATTN: HALLOWAY**
**SUBJECT: PRELIMINARY RUN 404**

**UNEXPLICABLE SPIKE IN CALORIMETER DATA. STANDARD MODEL VIOLATION CONFIRMED. REPEATABLE EVENT.**
**MISSING ET VECTOR MAGNITUDE:**
**4.119283001 GEV**

The world stopped.

The ticking of the grandfather clock in the corner ceased. The rain against the glass vanished. There was only the number.

**4.119283001.**

It wasn’t close. It wasn’t an approximation. It was exact.

"To the ninth decimal point," Halloway whispered. The awe in his voice broke the tension, replacing it with something far more terrifying. "Do you understand the statistical impossibility of that, James? It’s not finding a needle in a haystack. It’s guessing the atomic weight of the needle from a separate continent."

James looked up. He felt lightheaded, as if gravity had just decided to loosen its grip on him specifically. "They found it. They found the thread."

Halloway collapsed into his chair, the leather groaning. He reached for his pipe, but his hands were shaking too much to pack the tobacco. He gave up and let it clatter onto the desk.

"They thought the machine was broken," Halloway said, his eyes wide behind the thick lenses. "They stopped the run twice. They calibrated the magnets. They checked the software. But the ghost kept showing up. Exactly where you said it would be."

Halloway looked at James, really looked at him, for the first time in three years. He wasn’t looking at a student anymore. He was looking at something dangerous.

"You realized," Halloway said softly, "that if this is true… if your metric is correct… then the Standard Model isn't just incomplete. It’s a shadow play. You’ve mathematically proven that position is an illusion."

"It’s just time, Professor," James said, his voice surprisingly steady. "It’s just one particle, taking the long way around."

"Feynman joked about it," Halloway muttered, rubbing his temples. "Wheeler theorized it. But you… you went and did the bloody arithmetic."

The Professor stood up again, agitated now, pacing the small rug behind his desk. "Do you have any idea what this means? The faculty board wanted to suspend you this morning. I had a letter drafted." He gestured to the wastepaper basket. "Now? Now I have the Director of the SPS asking who the hell 'J. Adamson' is and why he knows the secrets of their machine better than the engineers who built it."

James sat back. A strange sensation washed over him. It wasn't triumph. It was the cold, hard clarity of the VAX-11/780 screen. The universe was computable. He had hacked the source code.

"What happens now?" James asked.

Halloway laughed, a short, sharp bark of disbelief. "Now? Now, James, we burn the suspension letter." He pointed a finger at the telex. "Geneva is terrified. They have a result that defies physics, and the only person on the planet who predicted it is a twenty-year-old boy who likes arcade games."

Halloway leaned over the desk, his face inches from James's.

"You weren't just right, James. You were precisely, terrifyingly right. You’ve turned the Department of Physics into a church, and you’ve just performed a miracle."

James looked back at the yellow slip of paper. The numbers didn't look like miracle work. They looked like coordinates.

"It’s not a miracle, Professor," James said, a small, tired smile touching his lips. "It’s just a glitch. I just found where the seam is."

Halloway stared at him, the fear in his eyes slowly giving way to a profound, reluctant respect. He reached into his drawer and pulled out a bottle of sherry and two dusty glasses.

"Glitch or not," Halloway said, pouring with a trembling hand. "God help us all. Because I think you just proved that we are all the same electron talking to itself."

He pushed a glass toward James.

"Drink, Mr. Adamson. You’re going to need it. The phone is going to ring in a minute, and I suspect it will be Switzerland."

As if on cue, the heavy rotary phone on the desk began to trill. It rang with a shrill, insistent violence that cut through the quiet room.

Halloway didn't answer it immediately. He raised his glass.

"To vindication," Halloway said. "However terrifying it may be."

James raised his glass. The amber liquid caught the grey light from the window. He thought of the single electron, zipping back and forth through the history of the universe, creating stars, planets, professors, and telex machines, just so it could eventually sit in this chair and toast itself.

"To the one," James said.

The phone continued to ring, demanding to be heard. James took a sip. It tasted like burning. It tasted like reality.
