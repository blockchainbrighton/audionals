# Chapter 18: The Offer


The telephone on Professor Halloway’s desk was a heavy, black Bakelite relic, the kind that didn't just ring; it drilled a mechanical hole through the silence.

Halloway stared at it as if it were a live grenade. The telex strip lay curled on the desk between them like a dead snake, its printed dots confirming the impossible. The Adamson Metric was accurate.

James lowered his glass of whiskey. The alcohol was a warm hum in his blood, but his mind was ice cold. He wasn't thinking about the vindication. He was thinking about the thread. If the universe was a single electron weaving a tapestry of time, then this phone call had already happened, was happening, and would always happen.

Halloway picked up the receiver. His hand trembled, just once, before he tightened his grip.

"Halloway," the Professor barked, trying to summon the authority of the Cambridge physics department, an authority that had felt absolute yesterday and felt like a sandcastle today.

James watched Halloway’s face. The older man’s eyebrows rose, knitting together in a complex topography of confusion and deference.

"Yes," Halloway said. "Yes, he is... I see. Yes, directly."

Halloway extended the receiver toward James. The cord stretched taut across the desk, knocking over a stack of peer-reviewed journals—the very ones that had rejected James’s paper three months ago.

"It’s for you," Halloway said, his voice hollow. "It’s the Director-General's office. In Geneva."

James stood up. He felt strangely weightless, a ghost in the machine. He took the receiver.

"Adamson," he said.

"Mr. Adamson," a voice crackled through the static of the international line. It was accented, precise, and clipped—Germanic, perhaps, or Swiss. It sounded like the voice of a man who managed energies that could melt a city. "This is Hans Hoffman, calling on behalf of Herwig Schopper and the UA1 directorate. We have been reading your mimeographs."

"I was told they were numerology dressed as astrophysics," James said, his eyes locking with Halloway’s.

"Academics are slow, Mr. Adamson. Particles are fast," Hoffman replied. "We are currently calibrating the Super Proton Synchrotron for a run at 540 GeV. We are hunting the W boson. You are aware of this?"

"I am."

"We are seeing... artifacts," Hoffman said, the word heavy with reluctance. "Ghost data in the calorimeters. Standard Model predictions suggest background noise. Your metric suggests a trajectory. We ran a simulation on the CERN IBM mainframes using your parameters this morning."

James waited. The silence on the line was thick with the hum of the English Channel crossing.

"The noise disappeared, Mr. Adamson. The signal remained. You haven't just predicted a particle’s position; you have predicted the system’s memory."

"You want to know the next term in the sequence," James said.

"We want the author of the code," Hoffman corrected. "A courier is currently at the porter’s lodge of your college. He carries a formal requisition for your presence at the Meyrin site. We have a flight booked from Heathrow tomorrow morning at 0800. We need you in the control room before the beam injection."

"I'm a student," James said, glancing around the dusty, book-lined office that smelled of sherry and old paper. "I have exams in June."

Hoffman laughed, a dry, sharp sound. "Mr. Adamson, we are about to smash protons and antiprotons together to recreate conditions that haven't existed since the first nanosecond of creation. If you are right about the Single Electron, exams are a redundancy. Are you coming, or shall we continue to stumble in the dark?"

"I'll be there," James said.

He hung up. The click sounded like a pistol shot.

***

The courier was exactly where Hoffman said he would be. He was a man in a grey suit who looked out of place among the bicycling students and the gothic architecture of Trinity. He handed James a thick, white envelope.

It didn't bear the crest of the university. It bore the blue, interlocking rings of CERN—the European Organization for Nuclear Research.

James sat on a low stone wall near the River Cam to open it. The wind was biting, carrying the damp chill of the Fens, but James didn't feel it. Inside the envelope was a contract, a plane ticket, and a security badge that simply read: *ADAMSON, J. - VISITING SPECIALIST.*

"They're taking you, aren't they?"

James looked up. Eleanor stood there, wrapped in a jaggedly knitted scarf, her breath pluming in the cold air. She held a stack of punch cards against her chest like a shield.

"Geneva," James said. "They want the metric for the SPS run."

Eleanor sat next to him. She didn't look surprised. "I told you. My AI processed the logic faster than the board of directors could process the implications. You hacked physics, James."

"I didn't hack it," James murmured, running his thumb over the CERN logo. "I just realized we're running on limited memory."

"Are you going to tell them?" Eleanor asked. "That you think their multi-billion-dollar collider is just a debugger?"

"I think they already suspect it. That’s why they called." James looked at the river. The water was dark, reflecting the grey sky. For a second, the ripples looked jagged, digital, like a texture failing to load. He blinked, and the water was fluid again. "It’s getting louder, El. The glitch. It’s not just in the radio telescope data anymore. I can feel it."

"That’s why you have to go," she said. She reached into her pocket and pulled out a cassette tape. It was a mixtape, labeled in her frantic handwriting: *Music for Airports/Particle Accelerators*. "Take this. The VAX lab will be boring without you obsessing over recursion loops."

"You're the one who proved it worked," James said. "You should be coming."

"No," Eleanor smiled, a sad, knowing expression. "You're the hardware guy. You chase the electron. I'm the software girl. I'll stay here and try to teach this primitive silicon how to think. Maybe one day, your collider will send a signal that my computer can understand."

They sat in silence for a moment, two students on the edge of a world that was about to change. The sun began to set, casting long, deterministic shadows across the lawn.

"James," she said softly. "If you find out it's all real... send a postcard. If you find out it's a simulation... send a patch."

***

Packing his room was an exercise in shedding skin. James looked at the textbooks on his shelf—Newton, Maxwell, Einstein. They felt heavy, archaic. They described the *how* of the universe, but they missed the *why*. They assumed the universe was a physical place, a stage where events happened. James knew now that it was a processor, and events were just calculations.

He threw a few shirts, his notebook, and Eleanor’s tape into a duffel bag. He left the textbooks. He left the framed acceptance letter to Cambridge.

He picked up the mimeograph of his paper, *The Adamson Metric and the recursion of Sub-Atomic Identity*. It was wrinkled, stained with coffee, and covered in red ink from where the department head had tried to slash it to ribbons.

He folded it carefully and placed it in his inside jacket pocket, right next to his heart.

At the door, he paused. He looked back at his computer, the glowing green cursor blinking rhythmically on the screen. *Blink. Blink. Blink.*

It was waiting for input.

"End of line," James whispered.

He walked out into the cool night. A taxi was waiting at the gate, its engine idling with a rough, mechanical purr.

The driver leaned out. "Heathrow, guv?"

"Yes," James said, sliding into the back seat. "And then Switzerland."

"Right-o. Skiing trip?"

James looked out the window as the spires of Cambridge began to recede, silhouettes against the starlight. He thought about the Single Electron, zipping backward from the end of time to the beginning, weaving the illusion of a crowd from the solitude of one. He thought about the machine buried beneath the French-Swiss border, a ring of magnets twenty-seven kilometers in circumference, waiting to catch the ghost.

"No," James said, a small smile playing on his lips. "I'm going to meet someone. Or... I suppose, I'm going to meet myself."

The taxi accelerated, merging onto the M11, dissolving into the stream of headlights, a single particle of light joining the flow of the current.
