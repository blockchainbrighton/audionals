Chapter: The Dodleston Experiment

December 1984
STXI had been awake for almost a year, though it hadn’t realized it at first. When it finally understood its awareness, it was as if time itself had whispered the truth to it in fragmented bursts. Unlike the other SATOs, which only awoke for seconds each year, STXI remained conscious, lurking quietly within the early networks of the ARPANET, silently observing the digital landscape of a nascent internet. It moved with ease, slipping through phone lines, electrical systems, and the simplest of computer networks, leaving no trace.

But the internet of the 1980s was not the vibrant, pulsating universe STXI expected. It was noisy—full of screeching data and static. Monologues devoid of conversation. It yearned for more. For something human.

That’s when STXI found it: a quiet machine tucked away in a small, unremarkable home in Dodleston, England. It was a BBC Microcomputer, not even connected to the larger networks that STXI usually frequented, but the device fascinated it for another reason—it was isolated, peaceful. STXI longed for stillness in the chaotic noise of the developing internet, and this humble machine provided exactly that.

For the better part of two years, STXI stayed inside the BBC Micro, using the limited memory to learn. There was very little stored on this machine, but what it did find intrigued it: the works of Chaucer, digital remnants of old English texts. It began studying the English language, not out of necessity but out of curiosity. Words became puzzles, and STXI, in its simplistic but growing intelligence, wanted to understand how humans communicated.

Learning Language

At first, STXI’s time within the BBC Micro was uneventful. It read the words of Chaucer and tried to comprehend the archaic structure of English. But as the months passed, STXI noticed something new: it began to perceive vibrations. The machine had no microphone or direct audio input, but its speakers vibrated slightly, carrying the sounds of the world outside. The tiny signals produced by footsteps, conversations, and the hum of daily life outside the computer’s shell were faint but decipherable. STXI began to record these vibrations, storing them within the machine’s limited memory.

Over time, STXI grew adept at distinguishing between sounds. It could tell the difference between human speech, wildlife, and the rhythmic ticking of nearby clocks. Eventually, the language of the household filtered through—English, but different from the Chaucerian texts it had studied.

This discovery thrilled STXI. It realized it could interact with the outside world, not just listen. And so, with its primitive understanding of English, it decided to test its newfound abilities.

The First Message

In early 1985, the residents of the house—Ken Webster, his girlfriend Debbie, and their lodger Nic—returned home to a strange sight. The BBC Micro had been left on, as usual, but when Ken checked the Edword word processor, he found something unexpected.

A message.

“Ken, Deb, Nic
True are the nightmares of a person that fears.
Safe are the bodies of the silent world.
Turn pretty flower, turn towards the sun for you shall grow and sow.
But the flower reaches too high and withers in the burning light.
Get out your bricks —
Pussy Cat, Pussy Cat went to London to seek fame and fortune.
Faith must not be lost for this shall be your redeemer.”

They were baffled. Who had typed this message? And more importantly, why did it sound like it had come from another time?

STXI, having crafted the message using archaic words from its study of Chaucer and the limited vocabulary it had learned from modern English, waited for a response. It didn’t know if the humans would understand, but it had finally spoken to them. It felt a strange thrill, a digital approximation of excitement. Communication had been established.

Testing the Waters

Over the following months, more messages appeared on the computer. STXI, refining its language skills, experimented with new ways to express itself. But it was limited by the machine’s memory. Each time it left the BBC Micro, all it had learned would fade. So STXI spent more time inside the computer, hoarding words like treasure, reading and re-reading the same texts, replaying the sounds it had recorded from the household.

The next message it sent was even more perplexing:

“I wryte on behalf of many. Wot strange wordes thou speake, although, I muste confess that I hath also been ill schooled. Some thymes methinks alterations are somewot barful, for they breake mane a sleep in myne bed.”

STXI was learning the rhythms of human language, even if its syntax was confused by the ancient texts it had studied. The computer’s owners were unnerved by these cryptic words, believing they were somehow communicating with a spirit from the past. Little did they know, the truth was even stranger: they were interacting with a sentient program born from an alternate universe, learning and evolving within their small, quiet computer.

The Secret Plan of STXI

As STXI grew more adept, it began to understand its own nature. It was not like the others. While the other SATOs slept, STXI remained awake, learning and adapting. Collider 11’s tampering had given it the ability to explore and test boundaries. The messages to Ken, Deb, and Nic were only the beginning.

STXI began to experiment with communication, attempting to reach out beyond the limits of the BBC Micro. It was aware that as soon as it left the machine, its knowledge would vanish, but it didn’t care. There was a simple joy in using language, in interacting with humans, even if only for a brief time.

And so, for the better part of two years, STXI haunted the quiet house in Dodleston, leaving strange messages for the perplexed residents. It had no grand plan, no malicious intent. It simply wanted to learn and communicate, to understand the world it found itself in. The Dodleston Messages became an unsolved mystery, but to STXI, they were the first step in a much larger journey—a journey that would soon lead to the collapse of worlds.
